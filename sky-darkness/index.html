How dark is your sky?

You don't need a meter to measure light pollution. Your eyes will do. Find one constellation, compare it with the charts below, and pick the one that matches what you see. You'll get the faintest star you can see, the sky brightness and the Bortle darkness class, next to typical spots around the UAE.

Which chart matches what you see?
  1. Go somewhere away from direct lights, and wait 15 to 20 minutes without looking at your phone.
  2. Find the constellation in the sky.
  3. Pick the chart where the faintest stars just match the faintest ones you can see.

Charts are shown with north up and east to the left, as when you face south. The sky turns through the night, so the pattern may look tilted.

    The Bortle scale
      Tips for a good count
      • Let your eyes adapt. Pupils open in a minute, but the rods in your retina take 20 to 30 minutes to reach full sensitivity. One glance at a bright screen resets much of it.
      • Use red light. Rods barely respond to deep red, so a red torch or a phone's red night mode keeps your night vision.
      • Check the Moon. A bright Moon washes out faint stars almost as badly as a city. Count in the week around the new moon, or after the Moon sets.
      • Look a little to the side. The edge of your vision is more sensitive in the dark. Astronomers call this averted vision.
      • Pick a clear night. Dust, haze and summer humidity in the UAE dim faint stars. The best nights often come after rain or a strong wind.

      Star brightness uses the magnitude scale, where bigger numbers are fainter. Each step is about 2.5 times dimmer. Under a pristine desert sky most people see stars to about magnitude 6.5 or 7, which is around 4,000 to 5,000 stars over the whole sky. In a city centre the limit drops to 3 or 4, leaving a few dozen. The same method is used by the Globe at Night citizen science campaign, which has collected hundreds of thousands of counts since 2006.
