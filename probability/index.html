Probability puzzles.

Our gut is bad at chance. These five puzzles fool almost everyone the first time, including people with maths degrees. Play each one, then let the computer run it thousands of times to see what really happens.

Puzzle 1

Monty Hall: stay or switch?

A car is behind one of three doors and goats are behind the other two. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door to show a goat. Should you switch?

Simulate 10,000 games
Always switchAlways stay

Switching wins 2 times in 3. Your first pick is right only 1 time in 3, and the host's choice doesn't change that. The other 2 in 3 chance all moves to the one door he leaves closed. It's clearer with 100 doors: you pick one, the host opens 98 goat doors and leaves one closed. Would you really stick with your first guess? Switching then wins 99 times in 100.

Puzzle 2

The birthday paradox.

How many people do you need in a room for a better than even chance that two share a birthday? Most people guess around 180. The answer is 23.

50.7% chance that at least two share a birthday

Try your own group

    The trick is counting pairs, not people. With 23 people there are 253 different pairs, and each pair has a 1 in 365 chance of matching. The exact chance multiplies the odds that each new person misses everyone before them: 364/365 × 363/365 × … This ignores 29 February and assumes birthdays are spread evenly. Real birthdays cluster a little, which makes a match slightly more likely.

    The gambler's fallacy.

    After five heads in a row, tails feels "due". It isn't. A coin has no memory. Predict each flip and watch what you do after a streak.

    The reveal: how long do streaks get?

    When people write down a "random" list of 100 flips, they switch sides too often and rarely include a run longer than 4. A real coin usually gives a longest run of 6 or 7 in 100 flips. Teachers use this to spot made-up homework. The same bias makes us see "hot streaks" in sport and "due" numbers in lotteries.

    Puzzle 4

    The base-rate fallacy.

    A test for a rare disease is 90% accurate. You test positive. How worried should you be? Most people, and many doctors in studies, say about 90%. The real answer is often far lower.

    Sick, tests positiveHealthy, tests positiveSick, missed by the testHealthy, tests negative
    – chance you're sick if you test positive

    Counting people instead of using percentages makes this easy. That's called natural frequencies, and the psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer showed it helps doctors get the answer right far more often. When a disease is rare, the healthy people wrongly flagged can outnumber the sick ones. That's why a positive screening test is usually followed by a second, different test.

    Puzzle 5

    Simpson's paradox.

    Treatment A wins for small kidney stones. It also wins for large ones. Yet treatment B looks better overall. Both can be true at once. Flip the view to see how.

    Treatment A (open surgery)Treatment B (keyhole)
    GroupTreatment ATreatment B
    Small stones81 / 87 (93%)234 / 270 (87%)
    Large stones192 / 263 (73%)55 / 80 (69%)
    All patients273 / 350 (78%)289 / 350 (83%)

    The data is from a real 1986 study of kidney stone treatments (Charig and colleagues, BMJ). Doctors gave the tougher large-stone cases to treatment A more often. Large stones fail more with any treatment, so A's overall score is dragged down by the hard cases it took on. When a hidden factor decides who gets which option, the combined numbers can point the wrong way. Always ask how the groups were chosen.
