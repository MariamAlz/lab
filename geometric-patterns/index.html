Islamic pattern maker.

The star patterns on mosque doors, mashrabiya screens and zellige walls can be drawn from plain tilings of squares, hexagons and octagons. Pick a tiling and a contact angle, and the stars grow by themselves. Weave the lines over and under, colour the shapes, and export the result for a print or a laser cutter.

The contact angle. In the 1920s the engineer Ernest Hanbury Hankin noticed how these patterns could be built. Take a tiling. From the middle of every edge, draw two lines into each tile, tilted by the same angle. Stop each line where it meets its partner from the next edge. Small angles give thin crosses, around 60 to 70 degrees gives classic stars, and large angles give wide rosettes. The offset splits each crossing point into two.

Symmetry groups. Every repeating flat pattern belongs to one of exactly 17 symmetry types, called wallpaper groups, proved in 1891. A square tiling gives p4m: it turns onto itself a quarter turn at a time and has mirror lines. Hexagon-based tilings give p6m, with sixth turns. Weaving breaks the mirror lines, because a mirror would swap over and under, and leaves the rotations.

Why there are no 10-fold tilings. You can't tile a flat floor with regular decagons and nothing else, and no repeating pattern can have 5-fold or 10-fold turns. Craftsmen in Iran and Morocco got around this with patches built around one big star. The decagon rosette here is one: a decagon ringed by ten pentagons. For laser cutting, export the plain SVG. It has only the lines, as thin strokes with no fill.
