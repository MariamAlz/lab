      Is your own DNS encrypted?

      Normally your phone asks "where is example.com?" in plain text, so your network or internet provider can see every site you visit. Encrypted DNS hides those questions. A web page can't reliably see how your device sends its DNS, so instead: switch it on, then confirm with Cloudflare's test page ↗ if you use 1.1.1.1.

      iPhone and iPad
      1. Install a DNS profile from your chosen provider (for example 1.1.1.1's app, or a profile from Quad9 or NextDNS).
      2. Open Settings, General, VPN & Device Management, DNS, and choose it.
      Android
      1. Settings, Network & internet, Private DNS.
      2. Choose "Private DNS provider hostname" and type one.one.one.one or dns.quad9.net.
      Windows 11
      1. Settings, Network & internet, your connection, Hardware properties, DNS server assignment, Edit.
      2. Set the DNS to 1.1.1.1 (or 9.9.9.9) and DNS over HTTPS to On (automatic template).
      Mac
      1. macOS needs a DNS profile, like iPhone, or a provider's app.
      2. Or turn on secure DNS in your browser (below).
      Chrome, Edge, Firefox
      1. Chrome and Edge: Settings, Privacy and security, Use secure DNS, pick a provider.
      2. Firefox: Settings, Privacy & Security, DNS over HTTPS, Increased or Max Protection.

      DNS was designed in 1983, when nobody encrypted anything. Its fixes arrived piece by piece: DNSSEC signs answers so they can't be forged, DNS-over-HTTPS and DNS-over-TLS hide the questions, and Encrypted Client Hello hides the last plain-text clue, the site name in the HTTPS handshake. This page checks which pieces a domain has switched on.
