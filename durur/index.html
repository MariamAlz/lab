Al Durur, the star calendar.

Before printed calendars, people in the Emirates and Oman counted the year in tens of days, starting from the rising of Suhail (Canopus) in mid-August. Each ten days is a دَرّ (durr). Farmers, fishermen and sailors knew what each one meant. Here is today's durr, the full year, and when the old season stars really rise where you are.

How it works

The count starts on 15 August. The year is split into four parts: the hundred days of الصفري (autumn), the hundred of الشتاء (winter), the hundred of الصيف (which in the old Gulf sense means spring), and sixty days of القيظ (high summer). That is 36 durur, or 360 days. The last five days before Suhail, six in a leap year, are المساريق, the "stolen" days that are left out of the count.

People speak of a durr by its running number inside its hundred: "دخل الخمسين" means the fiftieth day, the fifth durr, has begun. Because the count is tied to the solar year and not to the Moon, it falls on the same Gregorian dates every year, which made it a dependable guide for planting, fishing and travel.

The calculated dates use the Sun's position and each star's position for this year. A star counts as seen when it is at least 2° above the horizon (5° for a hazy horizon) while the Sun is still far enough below it, about 10° for the brightest stars and more for fainter ones. For Abu Dhabi this gives about 25 August for Suhail, close to the 24 August that the Emirates Astronomy Society announces.

Where sources differ

Most UAE sources start the count with the Suhail season on 15 August, but Suhail is first seen at dawn around 24 August. Some people start counting from the actual sighting, and Omani sea people traditionally started about 15 days later, in early or mid September.

Sources also differ on some star names. الأحيمر is the heart of Scorpius (Antares) in one account and Arcturus in another. الكليبين is described in different ways, so it is not calculated here. Some sources give القيظ 65 days by including the stolen days.

Some astronomers now say the old dates fit less well than they did. Summers are hotter, and the stars themselves drift slowly against the calendar, by about a day every 70 years, because the Earth's axis wobbles. Suhail does not change the weather. It only marks the time of year.
