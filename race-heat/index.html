Drink and salt. In warm races, drink to thirst at every water station and don't skip the early ones. For races over about 90 minutes, take electrolytes or a sports drink as well as water, because you lose a lot of salt in sweat. Practise this in training first. Stop and get help if you feel dizzy, confused or stop sweating.

Two ways to predict. The Riegel formula says time grows a bit faster than distance: T2 = T1 × (D2 ÷ D1)1.06. VDOT comes from Jack Daniels and Jimmy Gilbert. It turns your race into an oxygen cost, then asks what pace you could hold for each new distance. Both assume you have trained for that distance. A 5K runner who never runs long will be slower in a marathon than either says.

The heat rule. Add the air temperature and the dew point, both in °F. At 100 or less, there's no need to change your pace. From 101 to 180, slow down by 0.5% up to 10%. Above 180, don't race hard. This is a coaches' rule of thumb popularised by running coach Mark Hadley, not a lab result, so use it as a guide.

Why dew point? Dew point tells you how much water is in the air. Sweat only cools you when it evaporates, and in humid air it drips off instead. A 24°C morning with a 22°C dew point on the coast is much harder than a dry 30°C afternoon in Al Ain.

Forecasts come from Open-Meteo and reach about 16 days ahead. For races further out, type in the expected values by hand. These numbers are an estimate, not medical advice. If you have a heart condition or are new to racing in heat, check with a doctor first.
