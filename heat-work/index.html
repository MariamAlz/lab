    Heat exhaustion

    • Heavy sweating, cold clammy skin
    • Dizziness, headache, feeling sick
    • Muscle cramps, weakness, fast pulse
    What to do
    • Stop work and move to shade or AC
    • Loosen clothes, cool with wet cloths
    • Sip water or an electrolyte drink
    • No better in 30 to 60 minutes? Get medical help

    Heat stroke · emergency

    • Confused, slurred speech, acting strangely
    • Hot skin, may stop sweating
    • Fainting, fits, collapse
    What to do
    • Call 998 for an ambulance now
    • Cool them fast: shade, water on skin, fan, ice packs at neck, armpits and groin
    • Don't give drinks if they aren't fully awake
    • Stay with them until help arrives

    Why WBGT and not the temperature? Your body cools by sweating, and sweat only works if it can evaporate. The wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) adds humidity, sun and air together into one number, which is why occupational heat rules use it. The heat index only adds humidity, and it assumes shade.

    How it's worked out: the heat index uses the NOAA (Rothfusz) formula. WBGT is estimated, not measured. In the shade it is 0.7 × wet-bulb + 0.3 × air temperature (Stull's wet-bulb formula). In the sun it moves towards the Australian Bureau of Meteorology approximation, 0.567 × air temperature + 0.393 × vapour pressure + 3.94, in proportion to the forecast sunshine. Wind is not included, so a strong breeze can make it feel a little easier. Work/rest and water follow general occupational heat guidance (the kind used by safety bodies and the military).
