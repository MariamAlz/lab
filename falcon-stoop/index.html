How a falcon dives.

A peregrine falcon in a full dive, called a stoop, is the fastest animal on Earth. This simulation shows how it gets there: folding its wings to cut drag, picking a steep angle, then pulling out at several times the force of gravity. Change the bird, its shape and the dive, and compare it with a skydiver and a pigeon.

Drag grows with the square of speed. The falcon speeds up until drag equals the part of its weight pulling it along the dive, which is its terminal speed. Folding the wings shrinks the area facing the air and smooths the shape, so drag drops and the terminal speed rises. A steeper dive puts more of the weight to work. Heavier birds of the same shape are faster, because weight grows faster than frontal area.

What the records say

The fastest dive on record is about 389 km/h (242 mph), by a trained peregrine called Frightful released from a plane at about 5,200 m by falconer and skydiver Ken Franklin in 1999. Guinness World Records lists the peregrine's normal hunting stoop at about 320 km/h. Some scientists question the faster figure, because wild falcons rarely dive from that high.

Field measurements are lower. A trained gyrfalcon tracked by Vance Tucker and colleagues in 1998 reached 52 to 58 m/s (about 190 to 210 km/h) in dives from up to 500 m, and often added drag on purpose to control its speed. Tucker's models of an ideal falcon put the top speed in a long vertical dive at roughly 320 to 400 km/h, close to what this simulation gives for a fully tucked bird.

The pull-out

To turn a dive into level flight, the falcon has to curve its path. The tighter the curve and the higher the speed, the bigger the force: it grows with the square of speed. Wind-tunnel and field work by Christoph Brücker's group measured pull-outs of about 3 g in a controlled dive, and predicted close to 10 g at full stoop speed. A trained fighter pilot in a g-suit manages about 9 g. The falcon spreads the pull-out over a longer arc when it can, and its lift limits how hard it can turn.

Speed is not just for show. A 2018 computer study by Robin Mills and colleagues found that a high-speed stoop gives the falcon more turning force, which helps it catch agile prey that dodges at the last moment.

Falconry in the UAE.

Falconry is one of the oldest traditions of the Arabian Peninsula. Bedouin families trapped wild falcons on their autumn migration, trained them over the winter to hunt houbara bustard and hares for the family table, and often released them again in spring. The bond between falconer and bird, built on patience and trust, was valued as much as the hunt.

In 2010, UNESCO added falconry to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, in a joint nomination led by the UAE with other countries. The listing has since been extended several times and now includes many countries on several continents.

Today most falcons in the UAE are captive-bred, which protects wild populations. Falcons travel with their own passports under the CITES wildlife trade rules, and Abu Dhabi has a hospital just for falcons. Since 1995 the Sheikh Zayed Falcon Release Programme has returned hundreds of sakers and peregrines to the wild along their migration routes. Sakers usually hunt with fast low chases, while peregrines are the ones famous for the high stoop.
