SIM swap protection.

Your phone number unlocks more than calls. Banks, email and apps send login codes to it. In a SIM swap, a criminal gets your number moved to their SIM, and every code goes to them. This page shows the warning signs, a checklist to close the gaps, and a plan for the first few minutes. Your ticks stay in this browser. Nothing is uploaded.

What it is

SIM swapSomeone convinces your carrier, in a shop or on the phone, to put your number on a new SIM or eSIM. They often use a fake ID or details found online.
Number transfer fraudSomeone asks another carrier to move your number to an account they control. The effect is the same: your number now rings their phone.

Either way, they then reset your email, banking or messaging accounts with codes sent by text.

Warning signs

  • Sudden "No service" or "SOS only"Your phone loses signal where it normally works, and a restart doesn't fix it.
  • A message about a SIM change you didn't ask forSome carriers send a text or email when a SIM or eSIM is replaced. Take it seriously.
  • You are logged out of accountsEmail, WhatsApp or banking apps ask you to sign in again, or say your password changed.
  • Login codes you didn't ask forSomeone may be testing which accounts they can reset.
  • Friends get odd messages from youA hijacked WhatsApp is often used to ask your contacts for money or codes.

Move two-step login off SMS

Go account by account. Open each service's security settings and look for an authenticator app, passkey or security key. Tick it when done.

How it works: your number is just a record at the carrier that says which SIM it belongs to. Change that record and every call and text follows it, even though your phone is still in your hand. That is why an authenticator app or passkey, which lives on your device, is safer than a code by text.

If it happens: the first minutes

  1. Call your carrier from another phoneUse the official number from the carrier's website or a bill. Say you think your SIM was swapped and ask them to block it and give you a new one.
  2. Call your bankUse the number on the back of your card. Ask them to freeze cards and online banking until you are back in control.
  3. Change your main email password firstDo it from a computer or a phone you trust. Sign out all other sessions, then check the recovery phone, recovery email and forwarding rules haven't been changed.
  4. Then the restApple or Google account, WhatsApp, social media, shopping and crypto. Turn on an authenticator app as you go.
  5. Warn your contactsTell family and friends not to answer money or code requests from your number for now.
  6. Report it to the policeUse your emirate's police e-crime reporting service or app. Keep a record of times, messages and reference numbers from the carrier and bank.
