Why the sea goes up and down
How it works

The Moon's gravity pulls hardest on the side of Earth facing it and weakest on the far side. That difference stretches the oceans into two bulges, one under the Moon and one opposite. Earth turns under both bulges every day, so most coasts get two highs and two lows, about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.

The Sun does the same, a bit less than half as strongly. At new and full moon the two pulls line up and add: spring tides, with the highest highs and lowest lows. At first and last quarter they pull at right angles and partly cancel: neap tides, with a small range. The biggest tides usually arrive a day or two after the new or full moon, because the sea takes time to respond.

The Arabian Gulf is shallow and almost closed, so its tide sloshes around fixed "pivot" points where the range is nearly zero. That's why the tide at Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Ras Al Khaimah can differ by hours. Kalba and Fujairah face the open Gulf of Oman and follow a different rhythm.

These numbers come from the Open-Meteo marine model, not a tide gauge. Times can be off by 30 minutes or more and heights by tens of centimetres, especially inside creeks and lagoons. For boating, fishing or anything safety related, check official tide tables from the port or coastguard.
