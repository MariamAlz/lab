How VSEPR works. Count the groups of electrons around the central atom. Each bonded atom is one group, even with a double or triple bond, and each lone pair is one group. Two groups sit in a line, three in a triangle, four in a tetrahedron, five in a trigonal bipyramid and six in an octahedron. That is the electron geometry. The molecular shape is what you see if you look only at the atoms.

Why lone pairs squeeze. A lone pair is held by one nucleus, so it spreads wider than a bonding pair and pushes the bonds closer together. Methane has the perfect tetrahedral 109.5°. Ammonia, with one lone pair, closes to about 107°. Water, with two, closes to about 104.5°.

Polar or not. Each bond is a small arrow pointing to the atom that pulls electrons harder, its electronegativity (Pauling scale). Add the arrows. If they cancel and the lone pairs are spread evenly, the molecule is nonpolar, like CO2 or CH4. If not, one end is slightly negative, like water. That's why water dissolves salt and oil doesn't mix with it.

Angles shown are measured values for well-known molecules and typical values for the rest. VSEPR is a simple model. It gets most shapes right, but heavier atoms and unusual bonding can bend the rules.
