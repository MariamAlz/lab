Brain tricks.

Five short tests that psychologists have used for decades to study attention and memory. Each takes a minute or two. You'll see your result next to the range most adults score, and a short note on what the test shows about how the brain works.

Not a diagnostic test. These are for fun and curiosity. Your device, screen, tiredness and practice all change the scores. If you're worried about your memory or attention, talk to a doctor.

The Stroop test.

Pick the ink colour of each word, not what the word says. Some words match their colour and some don't. You'll do 24 words. Go fast, but try not to make mistakes.

Reading is so automatic for adults that you can't switch it off. When the word says one colour and the ink shows another, your brain has to block the word before it can name the ink. That extra time is the interference cost. J. R. Stroop described it in 1935. It's smaller in a language you read less fluently, so try it in both English and Arabic.

Reaction time.

Simple: tap the box, or press space, as soon as it turns green. Choice: a dot appears on the left or the right, and you tap that side (or press the left or right arrow key). Five tries each.

Light hits your eye, the signal travels to the visual cortex, a decision is made, and a command runs down to your finger. That chain takes about a quarter of a second. Adding a choice adds decision time: Hick's law says it grows with the number of options. Touchscreens and wireless keyboards add 20 to 100 ms of their own, so compare yourself on the same device.

Change blindness.

Two versions of the same scene flicker back and forth with a blank flash in between. One thing is different. Tap it when you spot it.

Without the blank flash, the change would jump out, because your eyes are wired to notice motion. The flash hides that signal, so you have to compare the scenes from memory. It turns out we store far less of what we see than it feels like. Ronald Rensink and colleagues showed in 1997 that people often need many cycles, sometimes a minute or more, to find a large change. The scenes here are drawn fresh by your browser each time.

Digit span.

Digits appear one at a time. Then type them back. Forward means in the same order. Backward means in reverse, which is much harder. The list grows by one each time you get it right. Two misses at the same length ends the test.

George Miller's famous 1956 paper called it "the magical number seven, plus or minus two". Most adults hold about 6 or 7 digits forward and 4 or 5 backward. Reversing the list needs you to hold it and work on it at the same time, which is what psychologists call working memory. Grouping digits, like a phone number, helps you hold more.

Corsi blocks.

The blocks light up one by one. Then tap them in the same order. It starts with 2 and grows by one each time you get it right. Two misses at the same length ends the test.

Press start when ready.

This is the visual partner of digit span. Philip Corsi designed it in 1972 with nine wooden blocks on a board. It measures how many places you can keep in mind, without words to help. Adults usually manage about 5 or 6. Most people find it a little shorter than their digit span, because we can't silently repeat locations the way we repeat numbers.
