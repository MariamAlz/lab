Arabic music is built on maqamat: scales made of small note groups called ajnas, some with notes that fall between the keys of a piano. Pick one of the eight common maqamat to hear it, see how it splits into ajnas, and play any note, quarter tones included, on a virtual oud neck.

Often heard in

    Quarter tones, roughly. The half-flat notes here sit exactly halfway between two piano keys, 50 cents from each. That is the usual way to write them down. Real players place them by ear, and the E half-flat of Rast in Cairo is a little different from the one in Aleppo or Baghdad. Treat these as a close sketch, not the final word.

    How the sound is made. Each pluck is a burst of random noise fed into a short delay loop that keeps averaging neighbouring samples. That is the Karplus–Strong method from 1983. The loop length sets the pitch, and the averaging softens the tone as it rings, much like a real string.

    Mood words are how listeners often describe each maqam, not rules. Song examples are from the MaqamWorld reference site. Long songs usually move through several maqamat, so the named maqam is where they start or spend most time.
