Why the orbit speed changes through the year

Earth's orbit is a slightly squashed circle. We're closest to the Sun in early January (147.1 million km) and furthest in early July (152.1 million km). Closer in, gravity pulls harder and Earth speeds up, so you move about 1 km/s faster in January than in July. The shape is exaggerated here so you can see it.

So why don't we feel it?

You only feel changes in speed or direction, not speed itself. Earth's spin and orbit are so smooth and steady that everything around you, the air, the sofa and your tea, moves along with you. It's the same reason you can pour a drink on a plane at 900 km/h.

The spin is fastest at the equator (1,670 km/h) and zero at the poles. In the UAE, at about 24°N, it's around 1,525 km/h. That spin is why rockets launch eastward, and why the UAE's lower latitude gives a small free boost compared with Europe.

These speeds don't simply add up

Each motion points in a different direction, and the directions change through the day and the year. The spin even runs backwards against the orbit at midnight and forwards with it at noon. So the true total is not the sum of the numbers. Measured against the afterglow of the Big Bang (the cosmic microwave background), the Sun moves at about 370 km/s towards the constellation Leo, and the Milky Way as a whole at about 550 km/s.

Earth's spin and orbit come from precise orbit maths for today's date. The galactic and cosmic speeds are measured values with uncertainty of several percent.
