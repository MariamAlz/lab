Paw-safe walks.

Dogs walk barefoot. On a sunny UAE afternoon the road can be 25 to 30°C hotter than the air, hot enough to burn paw pads in under a minute. This estimates the surface temperature from live sun, air and wind for your area, and shows which hours in the next day are safe for a walk.

Best times to walk

    The 7-second test. Put the back of your hand flat on the ground in the sun. If you can't hold it there for 7 seconds, it's too hot for paws. Tap the circle to count.

    Heat-stroke signs in dogs
    • Heavy panting that doesn't settle in the shade
    • Thick drool, very red or pale gums, bright red tongue
    • Weakness, stumbling, lying down and refusing to move
    • Vomiting or diarrhoea
    • Confusion, collapse or seizures

    If you see these: move the dog into shade or air conditioning, pour cool (not ice-cold) water over the body, especially belly and paws, put a fan on them, offer small sips of water, and call a vet straight away. Heat stroke can be fatal even after the dog seems to recover. Flat-faced breeds, puppies, old, overweight and dark-coated dogs are most at risk.

    How the estimate works. The surface is treated as a slab in balance: sunlight it absorbs (95% for asphalt) goes back out as heat into the air (faster in wind), heat radiated to the sky (less under cloud), and a share into the ground. That gives a steady-state temperature for each hour, which is then smoothed with a 3-hour lag because roads warm up and cool down slowly. On a 25°C sunny day it gives about 52°C for asphalt, close to the figure vets often quote from measurements.

    It is an estimate. Shade, building reflections, colour and fresh tarmac change things a lot, and air heat matters too: even on grass, a long walk in 35°C air can cause heat stroke. Weather from Open-Meteo forecasts. Always do the hand test.
