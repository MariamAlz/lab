Myth check: is a black robe always hotter? In 1980, Shkolnik and colleagues measured Bedouin robes in the Negev desert (Nature, vol. 283). Black robes got about 6°C hotter on the outside than white ones. Yet the heat reaching the body was about the same. The hot black cloth warms the air inside, the warm air rises and leaves through the opening, and wind pumps the loose robe like a bellows, carrying the heat away before it reaches the skin. The catch is the word loose. Switch the fit to "fitted" above and black pulls clearly ahead, because there's no air gap to carry heat away.

How the estimate works. The sun-facing side of the cloth gets about 40% of the sunshine falling on flat ground. It absorbs a share set by its colour: about 90% for black, 30% for white. It sheds that heat by wind on the outside, by radiation, and by air moving through the gap inside. Thin, open weaves like chiffon let more air through but also let some sunlight straight onto the skin, and light colours let more through than dark ones. Skin is taken as 34°C.

This is a simple energy-balance model to compare fabrics, not a lab measurement. Real numbers depend on how you move, sweat, shade and the exact fabric. Weather from Open-Meteo.
