Alert levels

    A HotSpot is how far the sea is above the warmest monthly average for that exact spot. Degree Heating Weeks add up HotSpots of 1°C or more over the past 12 weeks. So 4 DHW could mean 4 weeks at 1°C too hot, or 2 weeks at 2°C. Because each spot is compared with its own normal summer, the alerts already allow for local corals being used to the heat.

    Why corals bleach

    Reef corals live with tiny algae inside their tissue. The algae feed the coral through photosynthesis and give it its colour. When the water stays too hot, the partnership breaks down and the coral expels the algae, leaving its white skeleton showing through clear tissue. A bleached coral is not dead yet. If the heat eases within a few weeks it can take the algae back and recover. If not, it starves.

    The southern Gulf has some of the most heat-tolerant reef corals on Earth. They live through summers where the sea passes 34°C, temperatures that would kill most corals elsewhere. Scientists study them for clues to how reefs might cope with a warming ocean. But they are already near their limit: major bleaching events in 1996, 1998 and 2017 killed much of the coral off Abu Dhabi and Dubai. The east coast, on the Gulf of Oman, is a little cooler and sees cold upwellings.

    What divers and snorkellers can do
    • Don't touch, stand on or kick coral. Practise your buoyancy before reef dives, and keep fins high.
    • During an alert, stressed corals are fragile. Dive gently and keep groups small.
    • Use mineral sunscreen or a rash vest rather than lots of chemical sunscreen.
    • Report bleaching you see, with a photo, depth and location, to citizen science projects such as CoralWatch or to the local environment authority.
    • Choose operators who use mooring buoys instead of dropping anchors on the reef.
