When and where was this photo taken?

A shadow is a clock. Its length tells you how high the sun was, and its direction tells you where the sun was in the sky. Draw an upright object and its shadow on a photo, and this checks which times of day fit a claimed place, or which places on Earth fit a claimed time. Your photo stays on your device.

How it works. An upright object of height h casts a shadow of length L on flat ground when the sun is at height atan(h ÷ L) above the horizon. The same sun height happens twice a day, once in the morning and once in the afternoon, so direction is what tells them apart. Across the whole planet at one instant, the places where the sun has a given height form a ring around the point where the sun is straight overhead. That ring is the band on the map.

What goes wrong. A photo squashes the ground. A shadow pointing away from the camera looks much shorter than it is, while one running across the frame keeps its length. The tilt slider corrects for that, assuming a simple camera with little lens distortion, so prefer shadows near the middle of the picture. Sloping ground, a leaning pole, or a shadow falling on a wall or kerb all break the maths. Wide-angle and phone lenses bend lines near the edges. If you know the real height and shadow length (from a map, a satellite image, or a standard object like a door), type them in: that beats any drawing. Sun maths here is good to a few hundredths of a degree and ignores atmospheric refraction, which only matters within a few degrees of the horizon.
