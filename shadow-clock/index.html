Shadow clock.

Stand a straight stick upright in the sun and measure its shadow. From those two numbers you can work out how high the Sun is, roughly what time it is, and even how far north you are. It's how people kept time and found their way long before clocks and GPS.

Find east and west: the shadow-tip method
  1. Push a stick upright into flat ground in the sun.
  2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a stone. This first mark is west.
  3. Wait 15 to 20 minutes and mark the new tip.
  4. Draw a line from the first mark to the second. It points roughly east. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right, and you're facing north.

It works because the Sun moves east to west, so the shadow moves west to east. It's most accurate around midday and near the equinoxes. In early morning or late afternoon in summer it can be off by 10° or more. Marking the tip at two times when the shadow has the same length, before and after noon, gives an exact east-west line.

Before GPS

The Sun at noon gives your latitude: its height above the horizon depends only on how far you are from the equator and the date. Sailors measured it with a sextant every day at noon. Arab navigators in the Indian Ocean used the same idea at night with stars and a simple wooden kamal, a card on a knotted string. Ahmad ibn Majid, the 15th century master navigator born in Julfar near today's Ras Al Khaimah, wrote guides full of these star heights.

Longitude is harder, because it needs an accurate clock. If you know the exact time when the shadow is shortest, the difference from noon at Greenwich tells you how far east you are: 15° for every hour. That's what the clock box above does. Measurements with a stick are usually good to a degree or two, about 100 to 200 km.
