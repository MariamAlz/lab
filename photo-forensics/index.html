Was this photo edited?

A shocking photo is going round the family group. Before you forward it, look for the fingerprints that editing leaves behind: patches that compressed differently, areas with the wrong grain, copied-and-pasted regions, and tags left by editing apps. These are hints, not proof, but they often tell a story.

Each part of the photo is saved again as a JPEG and the difference is shown, brightened. Areas that were last saved together should glow about the same. A patch that glows much more or less than similar surfaces nearby may have been added later. Edges and fine textures always glow more, so compare like with like.

This removes the picture and keeps only the fine grain from the camera sensor. A real photo has an even, fine grain all over. Pasted or painted areas often look smoother (darker) or have a different texture.

The photo is cut into small overlapping 8×8 tiles, each summarised by its main patterns (a DCT, like JPEG uses). Tiles with the same summary are sorted next to each other. When many matching pairs share the same shift, part of the photo was probably copied and pasted, a common way to hide or duplicate things. Plain sky and walls are skipped because they match everywhere.

    The honest limits

    Hints, not proof. Error levels and noise change naturally with edges, textures, sky and shadows, so a bright patch isn't automatically fake. Look for a region that behaves differently from similar surfaces next to it.

    Social media wipes evidence. WhatsApp, Instagram and X shrink and recompress photos and strip the metadata, which erases most of these traces. A forwarded photo usually looks "clean" either way. Ask for the original file, or search for older copies with a reverse image search.

    AI images are made in one go, so they often show no editing traces at all. These tools find edits, not whether something was generated.

    How JPEG gives edits away

    JPEG cuts a photo into 8×8 squares and throws away detail in each one, guided by a "quantisation table". Every time you save, the squares settle a bit more, and the error shrinks. Paste in something new, save once, and the new part has been squeezed fewer times than the rest, so it reacts differently when squeezed again. The table itself is a clue too: cameras, phones and editing apps each use their own.
